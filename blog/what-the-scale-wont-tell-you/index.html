Your 40s and 50s: What the Scale Won’t Tell You

November 2026

Why the number on your bathroom scale tells only part of the story in midlife.

A note from Amber

If you’re in your 40s or 50s and your body feels different even though the scale hasn’t moved much, you’re not imagining it. After 18 years in imaging, I can tell you the scale only answers one question: how much do you weigh? It can’t tell you what that weight is made of, or where it lives. That’s the part that matters most in midlife, and it’s exactly what I want to help you see.

— Amber, RT(R), Founder

Why the scale can mislead after 40

You can lose muscle without noticing. Age-related muscle loss starts earlier than most of us think. Harvard Health notes that after age 30, we can lose as much as 3% to 5% of our muscle mass per decade.⁠[1] Muscle is a big part of what keeps you strong, steady and energized, and losing it can slow your metabolism.⁠[2] Because the scale only shows one total, a little less muscle and a little more fat can look like “no change.”

Fat tends to move to the middle. Mayo Clinic explains that the hormonal changes of menopause make weight gain more likely around the belly than the hips and thighs, and that weight gain often starts during perimenopause.⁠[2] Harvard Health puts it plainly: even if you don’t gain weight, your waistline can grow by inches.⁠[3]

Not all fat is the same. Some of that midsection fat is visceral fat, which sits deep in the abdomen around organs like the liver and intestines. Harvard Health links visceral fat to conditions including heart disease.⁠[3] Mayo Clinic adds that extra weight around the belly raises the risk of heart and blood vessel disease and type 2 diabetes.⁠[2] You can’t see or pinch visceral fat, and your scale can’t find it either.

What a body composition scan shows

A Body Composition Scan on our GE Lunar Prodigy DEXA system uses a very low dose of X-ray to show what your body is made of, not just what it weighs. You lie on an open, padded table for a few minutes while the scanner arm passes over you. There’s no tunnel and no needles. Just wear comfortable clothes without metal.

I’ll perform your scan myself and walk you through your results in plain language, so you leave knowing what your numbers mean.

What it measures

  • Fat mass: total body fat, including where it’s stored
  • Lean mass: muscle, organs and other lean tissue
  • Visceral fat: an estimate of the deep belly fat around your organs
  • By region: your arms, legs and trunk, so you can see where changes are happening
  • Bone mineral content: a general look at the mineral in your bones

3 things you can start this week

  1. Lift something, twice a week. The CDC recommends muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week, working all the major muscle groups, along with 150 minutes of moderate activity like brisk walking.⁠[4] Bands, dumbbells or bodyweight moves all count.

  2. Spread your protein across the day. Harvard Health notes that some research suggests protein may work better for preserving muscle when it’s spaced across meals and snacks rather than loaded into one meal.⁠[5] A simple start: add a protein source to breakfast. Ask your provider or a dietitian what amount is right for you.

  3. Track trends, not the scale. Pick a few markers and watch them over months, not days: how your clothes fit, your waist measurement (Harvard Health suggests measuring at the level of the navel, in the same place each time⁠[3]), and how strong you feel. A repeat body composition scan shows whether you’re gaining muscle and losing fat, even when your weight stays the same.

For the men and partners reading

This isn’t only a women’s issue. Harvard Health notes that most men will lose about 30% of their muscle mass during their lifetimes.⁠[1] And bone loss isn’t just for women: up to one in four men over 50 will break a bone due to osteoporosis, according to the Bone Health & Osteoporosis Foundation.⁠[6] Many men assume they’re not at risk, but NIH notes that older men are at risk too.⁠[7] If you lift, train or just want to stay strong for the long haul, knowing your numbers helps you, too.

Did you know? Your bones count, too

Osteoporosis is called a “silent” disease because there are typically no symptoms until a bone breaks.⁠[7] In the five to seven years after menopause, women can lose up to 20% or more of their bone density.⁠[6] Our Diagnostic Bone Density scan of the hip and spine includes a radiologist report.
